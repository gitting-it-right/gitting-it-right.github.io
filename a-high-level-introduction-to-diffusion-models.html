A high level introduction to Diffusion Models

Sreekar Garlapati
March 5, 2025
Updated on March 6, 2025

This article is a high level overview of diffusion models (for image generation). We will not be getting into the mathematical basis of the models here (You can refer to this excellent article for an in-depth introduction to diffusion models), or the nitty-gritty impelementation details (which you can find here).

What are diffusion models?

Diffusion models are neural networks that generate samples similar to a set of input images by incrementally adding noise to them and learning to denoise them.

In the above image, q is the forward process and our model is used to approximate the reverse process. Instead of modeling the inversion distribution function, the model estimates the noise that is added in the forward process. The loss is accordingly rewritten in terms of the predicted noise. The architecture used is a modified U-net model (Ronneburger et al.), with attention modules added before the downsampling and upsampling steps. 

The model learns how to denoise an image and after all time steps, it maps into the distribution of the training dataset. To generate a novel sample from the data distribution of the training data, we can use randomly generated array of pixel values and pass it to the model to get a new sample.

Faster inference in diffusion models

Diffusion models were introduced in the paper by Ho et al. (2020) titled Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM). Song et al. (2020) improved upon their model in their paper titled Denoising Diffusion Implicit Model (DDIM). 

The key idea of the paper was formulating a non-markovian sampling strategy that results in the same variational lower bound objective. The forward process remains the same while the reverse process can be done using a shorter sampling trajectory. The reverse process in DDPM involves sampling from a distribution involving the previous sample and the estimated noise given by the weights. Thus, we will need to generate samples for the every intermediate timestep to get a novel sample from the dataset. Sampling method in DDIM is deterministic, given the value at the previous time step in the trajectory, we use the estimate of the final sample (x₀)  and the denoising predicted by the model at that point to generate the sample at the current step. This allows us to generate samples using an arbitrary number of time-steps. The authors find that at shorter timesteps (~50), DDIM generates an image close to the image found at the 500th/1000th timestep.

In addition, since the process is deterministic, given the same starting sample, we will generate the same final image. Therefore, it also induces a latent space in the input sample space. Given two outputs of the DDIM procedure, we can generate an output that is a combination of the features of the two images by interpolating their inputs.

Improving the model

We highlight the key contributions of the paper Improved Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (IDDPM) by Nichol and Dhariwal (2021). A key distinction that needs to be made here is the difference between the noise schedule and the variance schedule. Noise schedule controls the rate of addition of noise in the forward process. In the reverse process, we also model it as a gaussian. The selected variance of the reverse process is dictated by the variance schedule. Why do they need to be different? We want our model to approximate the true posterior of the intermediate values of the reverse process. The last step's posterior should be the same as the training data distribution. This is an intractable distribution, it's variance depends on the dataset and therefore, does not have to equal the variance of the forward process. The authors of DDPM experimented with two choices of variance in the reverse process:

  1. Setting it to be the same as the variance in the forward process
  2. Deriving the mathematical form of the true posterior, conditioned on x₀ (true posterior unconditioned on x₀ is what we want our model to approximate) , and setting the variance equal to it's variance.

The authors found that they resulted in similar results with either of these choices. IDDPM improves this by setting a trainable parameter that interpolates these two variances. This results in model flexibility and when optimized, a closer approximation to the true posterior distribution. The authors have also experimented with a different noise schedule, where they use a cosine schedule where the variance is more gradually increased closer to t = 0 and t = T (as illustrated below). Introducing Guidance

DDPM provides no control on the outputs of the model, except that outputs will belong to the training dataset. DDIM gives one level of additional control because of the induced latent space in the input (noise). There are two approaches to adding more control: Classifier guided models and Classifier-free models.

Classifier Guidance

This method involves training a classifier on noisy images and the diffusion models separately. During inference, the gradient of log-likelihood of the noisy intermediate samples are used to drive the update towards the desired class.

Classifier-free Guidance

In Classifier-free guidance, we add the class information as input to the model and train a model that learns class conditioned diffusion and unconditional diffusion jointly. We train with a fraction of samples pdrop having a null-token as the class information (to learn unconditional generation) and the rest with encoded class information. During inference, a parameter w called guidance strength controls how strongly the user input guides the model, resulting in a controllable trade-off between sample diversity and adherence to inputs.

As we see in the update equation above, a higher w results in stronger adherence to the user-input class information and a lower w results in images closer to the training data distribution.
