LoRA: paper explained

Sreekar Garlapati
October 25, 2024
Updated on February 28, 2025

Alternatively, Understanding intrinsic dimension of LLM models and its implications.

Here I will be giving you a breakdown of the seminal paper LoRA: Low-rank adaptation of large language models by Hu et al. (2021) at Microsoft. The paper tackles a critical problem faced in practical uses of LLMs — expensive compute of fine-tuning large language models. First, I will introduce the related work done prior to the paper highlighting the key ideas which directly lead to the method used in LoRA. Next, I will explain the method introduced by this paper and go over the conclusions presented.

 

Intrinsic Dimension of a model

The concept of intrinsic dimension of a model was introduced in the paper Measuring the Intrinsic Dimension of Objective Landscapes by Li et al. (2018). The paper explores what the minimum number of parameters required are for a given loss function (or objective landscape). Let’s say we’re trying to find the optimal solution with D parameters θ*ᴰ for a loss function, then its intrinsic dimension dᵢₙₜ is the D-s, where s is the dimension of the subspace of the optimal solutions. For example, we pick an optimization problem where the loss is (x₁ — 1)² + (x₂ — 1)² and we try to find the solution in a ten dimensional parameter space. We will have a solution manifold of dimension 8 since coordinates x₃ through x₁₀ can be varied independently without affecting the loss. Hence the intrinsic dimension is 2. In our toy example it was easy to find the intrinsic dimension because the loss function was simple. In day-to-day ML applications, we cannot find the closed box formula for the optimal solution and thereby find the intrinsic dimension. In such cases, we define the best possible baseline solution we can find thorough fine-tuning as the baseline solution and its number of parameters as the intrinsic dimension. We define dᵢₙₜ₉₀ as the intrinsic dimension which can give us 90% of the performance of this baseline solution. The results shown in the paper point to neural networks being highly compressible, with dᵢₙₜ₉₀ being much lower than the baseline intrinsic dimension dᵢₙₜ₁₀₀. So, how exactly do we find dᵢₙₜ₉₀ for a neural network when we have our objective function defined in D parameters? We use a method to project a lower dimension parameter θᵈ to D parameters, such as multiplying it by a (random, fixed) Dxd matrix and optimizing the resulting loss landscape. We increase the dimension d until we hit a point where we get 90% of the performance of the baseline and this will be dᵢₙₜ₉₀. The paper explores other projection methods as well, such as using the Fastfood transform, which has better space and time complexities for projecting a vector into higher dimensions. The Fastfood transform is used extensively in the paper we discuss in the next section.

 

Intrinsic Dimension of adjustments in LLM fine-tuning

This paper by Aghajanyan et al. (2020) explores the intrinsic dimensionality of the fine-tuning adjustments in LLMs instead of the intrinsic dimensionality of the whole model which was explored by the paper discussed above. The experiments done indicate that SAID is typically lower than DID. The crucial takeaways from the paper are that both SAID & DID (for 90% of the baseline performance) are much lower than the number of parameters and that the intrinsic dimension decreases as the size of the pre-trained model increases. The latter result makes sense since the larger pre-trained model “understands” the language better, therefore achieving competitive performance on the downstream tasks is relatively easier. To summarize, we have observed that we can reach competitive performance in LLM fine-tuning by using lower dimensional parameters. Note that we are still using the Fastfood transform to project these into the full model parameter sized space, where we have a fixed, randomly-generated projection matrix. Can we do better than this? Let’s find out…

 

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)

Finally we come to the main paper which has seen widespread usage and has lead to a proliferation in fine-tuned models, LoRA. The above papers discuss the concept of intrinsic dimension of the models and the update parameters in model fine-tuning. This inspires the main hypothesis of the paper: that the update weight matrices in these models are of a low “intrinsic rank” and hence can be modeled by rank-decomposition matrices whose parameters are fully trainable! Applying LoRA to transformers

We have described the basic idea of LoRA above. How do we apply it to transformer models? The transformer models have several trainable parameters such as the query matrix Wq, key matrix Wk, value matrix Wv and the output projection matrix in multi-head attention models Wo for each layer. In addition there is a feed-forward layer with weights Wd. The approach in the paper uses independent LoRA matrix pairs for each matrix in the attention heads and leaves the feed-forward matrices unchanged. That’s it! This is a simple and straightforward method that is inspired by the previous works and improves upon them substantially.

What is the optimal r to be used in the weight matrices? The experimental results show that we improve upon the performance of fine-tuning the full model (Previous papers find dᵢₙₜ₉₀ which reaches 90% of this performance) with r being as little as 1 or 2! Further increases in r don’t result in significant gains and in some cases reduce the performance as well.

There are numerous advantages to using LoRA in LLMs. For a given downstream task, we can pre-compute the adapted weights by adding up the update matrices to the pre-trained models and thus have no additional inference latency, as opposed to some other methods such as introducing adapter layers which increases the latency. We can store the fine-tuned models for several downstream tasks by storing the pre-trained model and only the LoRA update matrices for each respective task (which are much smaller than the model). As r is increased to the rank of the optimal update matrices of the full model (including the feed-forward layer matrices and their biases), we will reach the expressiveness of fine-tuning the full model. Thus, LoRA can be seen as a generalization of full fine-tuning a model.
